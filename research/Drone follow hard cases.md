# Break the follower before real flights do

The hardest cases for an autonomous person-following drone are not top speed or a single difficult frame. They come from **losing the target and getting it back**. Full occlusion, out-of-view and similar-looking objects score lowest on every UAV tracking benchmark, and real users mainly report lock theft by look-alikes and drones stuck behind trees. For this simulator, the weakest link is the REACQUIRING state. It re-locks by position gate alone, behind a ByteTrack buffer that forgets a lost track after about one second, so a decoy standing at the predicted exit point will win. The second risk is that the simulator flatters the stack, for three reasons: Unity ground-truth depth draws 3 mm wires and glass perfectly where Depth Anything V2 will not, detections arrive without real-world latency, and Unity frames are cleaner than real camera images. Hard maneuvers matter too. A runner can brake harder than a tilt-limited drone can, and a target that doubles back closes an 8 m gap in under a second. Wind, sloped terrain and GPS loss add a further layer, mostly through the flat-ground and home-relative-height assumptions. The report ends with a prioritized table of 20 scenarios and 16 design gaps. Two conditions run through all of them: every scenario should also be run with injected ground-truth detections and with degraded depth, and a pass should be judged on wrong-person-follow time and minimum clearance, not just on whether the target stays in frame.

## Re-locking after occlusion is where this stack will fail first

Benchmarks agree on which cases are hardest. LaSOT names **fast motion, out-of-view and full occlusion as the three most challenging attributes**, and the best trackers reach only 0.304 success on full occlusion ([LaSOT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.07845)). On aerial video it is worse. On UAV123, full occlusion is the hardest attribute, with AUC 0.259–0.349 across ten deep trackers. In the same study **no tracker could reacquire a target that reappeared after leaving view**, and the authors note that none of them is designed for re-identification ([arXiv 2103.12924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.12924)). Real users report the same thing. DJI Air 3 owners describe the drone that "just gets stuck behind obstacles" and will "hover mindlessly, stuck" ([MavicPilots](https://mavicpilots.com/threads/disappointed-with-activetrack.143670/latest)). Skydio 2 owners report it "lost him completely and followed the wrong guy" ([SkydioPilots](https://skydiopilots.com/threads/disappointed-with-skydio-2.572/)).

ByteTrack sets the memory limit. It keeps an unmatched track as "lost" for **30 frames**, then deletes it. A new track needs a box scoring above 0.7 in two consecutive frames. It deliberately leaves out appearance in its second association pass ([ByteTrack](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2110.06864v2)). The Ultralytics version adds **no camera-motion compensation and no appearance model**, and `persist=True` must only be used on consecutive frames of the same stream ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track?h=track)). At 30 fps, the default buffer is one second. Walking behind a building or a tree line takes several seconds, so after any real occlusion the target comes back with a new ID. At that point the supervisor's position gate is the only thing linking it to the old target.

Position gating alone has a clear failure mode: a look-alike inside the gate. Similar-object attributes cost UAV trackers heavily (DTB70 similar-objects-around AUC 0.342–0.526) ([arXiv 2103.12924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.12924)). On a moving camera, the evidence favors two fixes, and neither is a generic ReID embedding. First, BoT-SORT's ablation found that **camera-motion compensation gave +1.6 IDF1 while ReID added only about +0.5** ([BoT-SORT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.14651)). Second, for single-target person following, an **online-learned, target-specific appearance model raised success from 20.2% to 94.9%** in one corridor test. It failed when a stored back view met a front view, and during long occlusions by similar-looking people ([arXiv 2309.11727](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11727)). Skydio's solution is to **track every nearby person, not just the subject**, so a distractor already has its own identity and cannot slip into the target's slot ([Skydio](https://medium.com/skydio/inside-the-mind-of-the-skydio-2-b1b78aa6dfa7)). A newer occlusion pipeline also **skips the Kalman correction step and inflates process noise during occlusion**. After 30 occluded frames it switches to appearance-only matching ([arXiv 2609.17427](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17427)). That is the reverse of a fixed-radius gate.

No source gives a validated horizon for how long a constant-velocity ground prediction stays trustworthy, so the simulator has to measure it. Two test structures from the literature fit. RPF-Search parameterizes occlusion as topographic (U-turns, hidden rooms) and dynamic: one occluder at a constant 1.0 m/s, one slowing from 1.5 to 0.8 m/s, or several occluders. It counts a re-identification only after the target has been seen for more than 5 consecutive frames ([arXiv 2503.02188](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.02188)). UnrealZoo varies crowd distractors at 0, 4 and 10, and a PID baseline's success fell from 0.34 to 0.14 across that range ([UnrealZoo](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20977v2)). The test this system needs most combines them. The target walks behind a wall, turns or stops while hidden, and comes out on the far side. Meanwhile a decoy in identical clothing comes out exactly where straight-line extrapolation predicts. A position-only gate fails this test by design.

The four-camera rig adds a related problem: **handoff across seams**. Commercial systems avoid it. Skydio 2 uses six 200° fisheye cameras for "triple coverage" and masks the propellers and body as invalid regions ([Skydio](https://medium.com/skydio/inside-the-mind-of-the-skydio-2-b1b78aa6dfa7)). Multi-camera trackers keep a global ID by correlating detections in known overlap regions ([topology-aware tracking](https://www.jaredwatkins.com/research/drone-detection/open-source/topology-aware-multi-camera-tracking/)). Four 90° cameras have little or no overlap. A person standing on a seam therefore appears as two truncated, low-confidence boxes whose bottom-centre foot points are biased, or appears in neither camera. Separate ByteTrack instances per camera give a new ID at every seam. Identity therefore has to live in the world-frame Kalman filter, and edge-touching boxes should be down-weighted, not trusted.

## Abrupt stops, reversals and loop delay matter more than top speed

Consumer follow drones advertise top speeds of 11.7 m/s (HoverAir X1 PRO, "track up to 42 km/h") to about 16 m/s (Skydio 2, at sea level with no wind) ([HoverAir](https://hoverair.com/pages/x1-pro-and-promax); [Skydio](https://support.skydio.com/hc/en-us/articles/5338292379803)). People are slower than that, but they can stop faster than a drone. A 2025 review measured **peak human deceleration of 9.5–12.0 m/s² in 180° change-of-direction tests**, and 7.6–9.4 m/s² in straight acceleration–deceleration tests ([Harper et al. 2025](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/57400/1/57400%20Harper%20et%20al.%20VOR.pdf)). A multirotor's horizontal acceleration is roughly g·tan(tilt), which is about 5.7–6.9 m/s² at a 30–35° tilt limit. When a runner stops from 6 m/s, the drone therefore needs about 3.0 m to stop while the runner needs about 1.6 m. That is roughly 1.4 m of overshoot before counting any loop delay (my arithmetic, not a sourced figure). A target that doubles back at 6 m/s toward a drone flying 6 m/s at 8 m follow distance closes the gap in about 0.7 s. The body-frame distance PID cannot respond in that time, so the supervisor needs a **minimum-separation guard** that brakes or climbs, regardless of what the PID commands. In one simulation, a perception-aware NMPC kept a target in view around an 8 m-radius curve at up to 8 m/s at 100% success, and at 85% success at 9 m/s, where the same controller without the visibility term scored 0%. Without that term it was perfect only up to 7 m/s ([PCVPC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.11063.pdf)). Explicit visibility objectives therefore extend the usable speed envelope by real margins.

The four cameras change what "losing the target from view" means. With 360° coverage, a fast lateral crossing does not drop the target out of the image. It moves the target into the side camera and starts TURNING. The bearing rate is about v/d. A crossing at 6 m/s at 3 m range is about 115°/s, which sweeps across a 90° camera in under a second (geometry, not sourced). So for this rig the close-crossing case mostly tests seam handoff and the yaw-rate limit, not FOV loss. If this system follows at a fixed angle relative to the target's heading, as PX4 Follow-Me does (FLW_TGT_FA, default 8 m distance and 8 m height) ([PX4 Follow-Me](https://docs.px4.io/v1.15/en/flight_modes_mc/follow_me.html)), a U-turn by the target will swing the drone around a roughly 25 m arc. A "lazy" follow angle that only changes after the target's heading has been stable for a while would avoid this.

Latency is the variable most likely to make simulator-tuned gains fail on the real drone. Khalifa University researchers measured a **139 ms lateral loop delay for vision-based position control**. The delay-free model predicted a gain margin of about 3.1. The delay-inclusive model predicted 1.4, and flight tests confirmed the vehicle was marginally stable there. The authors note that delay-free models have a "trivial optimum at infinite controller gains" ([arXiv 2209.01933](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.01933.pdf)). Their fix was simple: **advancing the reference by the measured delay recovered tracking performance** (about 5 cm RMSE). UnrealZoo shows the same effect from the perception side. One RL tracker's success went from **0.34 at 3 FPS to 0.62 at 10 FPS and 0.92 at 30 FPS** ([UnrealZoo](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20977v2)). The full chain on the real drone will likely total 150–400 ms: exposure and readout, YOLO, Depth Anything on four streams, the Kalman filter and PID, MAVSDK offboard transport, and PX4's inner velocity loop. This is an estimate to be measured, not a sourced figure. Unless the simulator injects that delay, its PID gains carry no information about real-flight behavior.

Wind shrinks the same envelope. Follow drones are rated for about 10.7–11 m/s of wind ([HoverAir](https://hoverair.com/pages/x1-pro-and-promax); [Skydio](https://support.skydio.com/hc/en-us/articles/5338292379803)). In an 8 m/s headwind, a 12–16 m/s airframe has only 4–8 m/s of ground speed left, so even a cyclist can outrun it upwind. Downwind, it has less authority to brake. Wind also causes **barometric height errors of "a few meters"**, especially after the drone slows from fast flight ([ArduPilot](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/airspeed-estimation.html)). Both PX4 EKF2 and ArduPilot EKF3 estimate wind from drag without an airspeed sensor ([PX4 EKF](https://docs.px4.io/v1.13/en/advanced_config/tuning_the_ecl_ekf.html); [ArduPilot](https://ardupilot.org/copter/docs/airspeed-estimation.html)). That estimate is available, and the supervisor should use it for warnings, for speed caps, and in the return-to-home energy budget. A single Skydio 2 field report describes it tracking in gusts above 40 mph with "some hairy moments" ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/2021/02/01/skydio-2-drone-strong-winds)). That is anecdotal evidence that flying past the rated wind limit is survivable, not that it is safe.

## Lighting, terrain and GPS break the assumptions the estimator relies on

Lighting breaks perception gradually, and it breaks the color-blob tracker first. UAVDark135 identifies **color distortion, low visibility and high sensor noise** as the defining night-time failures. Handcrafted trackers "easily lose accuracy and robustness" ([ADTrack](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2101.08446)). Skydio's list of hard visual conditions includes sun glare, high dynamic range, motion blur, reflections, fog and rolling shutter ([Skydio](https://medium.com/skydio/inside-the-mind-of-the-skydio-2-b1b78aa6dfa7)). A Skydio owner blamed a lost track on deep shadow and bright light in the same frame. Mini 4 Pro users found that a bright orange or red shirt "does great" ([SkydioPilots](https://skydiopilots.com/threads/disappointed-with-skydio-2.572/); [MavicPilots](https://mavicpilots.com/threads/dji-mini-4-pro-losing-subject-in-active-track-auto-mode-i-think-there-is-a-pattern-to-it.143416/latest)). That workaround amounts to admitting the tracker depends on color contrast. A backlit subject loses saturation and has no defined hue. Crossing a sun-to-shadow line shifts both brightness and color temperature while auto exposure catches up. A fixed HSV threshold fails in both cases. So the color-blob tracker should only adapt its thresholds while the lock is confident, and should need a person detection to confirm a blob. With four cameras, the sun will be in one of them, so a seam handoff into a glare-saturated camera can drop the target without any error. Per-camera image quality should feed the measurement covariance.

Small targets are a separate problem. The best VisDrone detectors reach only **about 39.4 AP**, and the organizers note that objects seen from a drone look different from ground-level views ([VisDrone-DET2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021W/VisDrone/papers/Cao_VisDrone-DET2021_The_Vision_Meets_Drone_Object_Detection_Challenge_Results_ICCVW_2021_paper.pdf)). With a 90° camera at 640 px inference width, a 1.7 m person is about 54 px tall at 10 m and 18 px tall at 30 m (pinhole arithmetic). At steep look-down angles, the person becomes a head-and-shoulders blob, and the bottom of the box no longer marks the feet.

That foot point is used for the ground projection behind both the Kalman filter and the position gate, and it assumes flat ground. If the feet are Δh above the assumed plane, the range error is Δh/tan(depression angle). At 8 m altitude and 10 m range, a 2 m rise in the slope adds about 2.5 m of error. At 4 m altitude and 15 m range it adds about 7.5 m (geometry; no source quantifies this for follow drones). Stairs and embankments turn this into a step. The Kalman filter sees a spurious velocity spike and commands a lurch. Height has the same flaw: PX4 Follow-Me's FLW_TGT_HT is **relative to home, not to the target or terrain** ([PX4 Follow-Me](https://docs.px4.io/v1.15/en/flight_modes_mc/follow_me.html)). If the drone holds 8 m above home while the target climbs about 6 m up a 20% grade, the drone ends up about 2 m above the target's head. The fix is to check the ground-plane range against a range estimated from box height (known person height). It also helps to hold height relative to the target, with an absolute floor above ground level from a rangefinder or the Depth Anything ground estimate.

GPS failures follow documented PX4 behavior. EKF2 accepts GPS only after quality checks hold for 10 s (eph < 3 m, at least 6 satellites, PDOP < 2.5), and in the SITL example it falls back to static-position mode after about 10 s without GPS ([PX4 EKF](https://docs.px4.io/v1.13/en/advanced_config/tuning_the_ecl_ekf.html)). Follow-Me needs a valid local position, and the vehicle fails safe if that is lost ([PX4 Follow-Me](https://docs.px4.io/v1.15/en/flight_modes_mc/follow_me.html)). Skydio owners report beacon GPS drifting "by a hundred feet or so" in canyons ([SkydioPilots](https://skydiopilots.com/threads/disappointed-with-skydio-2.572/)). The design rule is that **when PX4 takes control, the Python supervisor must notice**. A failsafe RTL or Land overrides offboard control, so the supervisor has to watch the flight mode through MAVSDK and move to its own RTH or HOLD state rather than keep streaming velocity setpoints. Compass faults are the other case to test. PX4's Gaussian-sum-filter emergency yaw reset can change heading suddenly, which rotates every camera's ground projection at the same moment.

## Ground-truth depth hides the obstacles that cause real crashes

The commonly reported follow-mode crash is a drone flying into a thin or swaying branch. Skydio says its navigation typically misses obstacles **"thinner than 1/2 inch"**, does not avoid a moving obstacle unless it is tracking it as a subject, and can mistake still water for open sky ([Skydio safety blog](https://skydio.com/blog/how-to-fly-safely-with-skydio-2)). A Mavic Air in ActiveTrack hit wind-blown branches after showing an obstacle warning ([MavicPilots](https://mavicpilots.com/threads/mavic-air-did-not-stop-before-crash-on-active-track.90004)). A Phantom 4 Pro veered sideways at speed toward trees about 7 s into a track, instead of hovering ([PhantomPilots](https://phantompilots.com/threads/glitch-with-active-track-sent-drone-toward-trees.135711/page-2)). HoverAir has largely given up on full avoidance. It replays the rider's path and builds an airframe meant to survive "hitting trees/poles/etc." ([DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2025/03/hoverair-x1-pro-gauntlet-tracking.html)).

Unity's ground-truth depth hides all of these. Depth Anything V2 would get them wrong. Zero-shot metric DA-V2 in natural scenes shows **AbsRel 0.56–1.48 and RMSE 7.6–13.7 m, and larger backbones did worse**. Fine-tuning brings this to about 0.16 AbsRel and 3.2 m RMSE ([WildCross](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.01475)). That zero-shot error is as large as the whole follow distance. Rescaling relative depth with VIO features gives about 0.14–0.26 AbsRel. It still has "chattering" from frame to frame near walls, and its scale drifts when the sky fills the frame. The authors added a 1 m collision radius to absorb the noise ([arXiv 2509.08159](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.08159)). Wire pixels have **about 4–5× the error of the whole image** (AbsRel 0.56–0.61 vs 0.13) ([UCorr](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14989)). DA-V2 is still wrong on a large share of glass and mirror pixels (δ1.05 of 0.58–0.64) ([arXiv 2408.06083](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06083v1)). From the air, DA-V2 shows "overall scale collapse" ([ISPRS 2026](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLIX-B4-2026/679/2026/)). Each camera will also have its own scale error, so the fused surround map will show phantom walls or gaps at the seams. Every avoidance test should therefore run in three depth modes: ground truth, degraded ground truth (per-camera scale random walk, flicker, sub-2-px structures erased, glass with no depth), and real DA-V2 run on the Unity RGB frames.

The reactive filter's structure is the second problem. Velocity limiting plus sidestep is a potential-field-style layer. Such layers stall in concave obstacles, oscillate when the detour is symmetric, and have no concept of line of sight. The literature solves this with predictive planning that treats occlusion as a cost. In Bonatti's cinematography planner, adding an occlusion term kept the target visible **86.9% of the time among 40 obstacles, against 75.3% with an obstacle term only** ([Bonatti et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1808.09563)). Its online version treats **unknown space as occupied** ([arXiv 1910.06988](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1910.06988)). Among recent trackers in a 100-obstacle simulation, failure rates ran from 40.6% (SF-Tracker) down to 6.75% (Eva-Tracker), and the authors note that corridor-only methods "often fail to keep the target visible" ([Eva-Tracker](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12549)). Skydio balances more than 40 objectives at 500 Hz, using the subject's *predicted* motion ([Skydio](https://medium.com/skydio/inside-the-mind-of-the-skydio-2-b1b78aa6dfa7)). A full planner is not required here. A smaller set of changes covers most of the gap: raycast the drone-to-target line through the surround map, commit to one detour side with hysteresis, keep map memory with decay, add a "go higher or go around" rule, and limit speed so that v·t_latency + v²/(2a) stays inside the *reliable* depth range. At 5 m/s² braking and 100 ms latency, that stopping distance is about 1.7 m at 4 m/s and 4.4 m at 6 m/s (kinematics). Four horizontal cameras also cannot see straight up or down. Overhangs, canopy and low branches therefore depend on map memory, because nothing will show them at the moment they matter.

## How to run the test campaign

The benchmark literature gives a ready-made scenario matrix. DAT combines maps, weather/lighting (day, fog, night, snow), tree and building density, a similar-looking distractor, and forward, lateral and yaw wind gusts. Its protocol is **4 initial relative angles × 10 episodes per map**, with tracking success rate (fraction of time the target is in view) as the main metric ([DAT](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00744v2)). The Unity scene already has decoys, wind and occlusion, so copying this structure costs little. Two results set limits on how to read the outcomes. A detector trained only on synthetic data matched real-data training on easy footage (97.0 vs 97.8 AP50) but fell to 67.8 vs 97.7 on night footage with on-screen artefacts. Domain randomization did not reliably help ([arXiv 2411.09077](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.09077v1)). YOLO performance on Unity renders is therefore not evidence of real-world performance. Each scenario should run twice: once with injected ground-truth (or noisy ground-truth) detections, which tests the supervisor, Kalman filter and control logic, and once with real YOLO, which tests perception. That split shows which component caused a failure.

Published active-tracking benchmarks report episode-level success but rarely report re-acquisition time, ID switches or smoothness, so those metrics have to be defined here. The proposed metric set, used in the table below, is: time-in-view, **time following the wrong person** (the most serious failure), wrong re-locks, re-lock time grouped by occlusion length (re-lock confirmed after 5 or more consecutive frames, as in RPF-Search), collisions and minimum clearance, stuck time, oscillation (sign changes per second in yaw or sidestep commands), minimum separation from the target, and the commanded speed while lock is lost. The pass thresholds below are proposed starting points, not values from the literature.

## Design gaps the research exposes

| Gap in current design | Why it matters (evidence) | Concrete fix |
|---|---|---|
| REACQUIRING re-locks by position gate only | No tracker re-identifies after out-of-view; look-alikes inside the gate steal the lock ([2103.12924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.12924)) | Score candidates by position likelihood × online target-appearance similarity; require a margin over the runner-up; collect appearance only while the lock is confident ([2309.11727](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11727)) |
| Fixed-size gate | Uncertainty grows during occlusion ([2609.17427](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17427)) | Gate radius follows Kalman covariance; skip correction while hidden; add hypotheses at the occluder's far-edge exit points |
| Only the target is tracked | Skydio tracks all nearby objects to tell candidates apart ([Skydio](https://medium.com/skydio/inside-the-mind-of-the-skydio-2-b1b78aa6dfa7)) | Keep world-frame tracks for every person; a distractor with its own history cannot be absorbed into the target |
| Per-camera ByteTrack, 30-frame buffer | IDs reset at seams and after ~1 s of occlusion ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track?h=track)) | One world-frame target memory independent of ByteTrack IDs; down-weight boxes touching image edges; rotate tracks by IMU yaw (in place of BoT-SORT camera-motion compensation) |
| Color-blob tracker as an equal option | Night/illumination changes break handcrafted trackers ([ADTrack](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2101.08446)) | Use color only as an appearance cue confirmed by a person detection; adapt thresholds only while locked |
| Ground-truth depth feeds avoidance | DA-V2 zero-shot RMSE 7.6–13.7 m; wires 4–5× worse; glass wrong ([WildCross](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.01475), [UCorr](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14989)) | Add degraded-depth and real-DA-V2 modes; run CI in degraded mode by default |
| Reactive filter without memory or line of sight | Occlusion-aware planning raises visibility 75%→87% ([Bonatti](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1808.09563)); reactive layers get stuck | Persistent surround map with decay; unknown = occupied; line-of-sight raycast; side commitment with hysteresis |
| No up/down depth coverage | Overhangs, canopy and low branches are outside all four camera views | Map memory plus a conservative vertical envelope under canopy; consider a downward rangefinder |
| Speed not tied to depth reliability | Stopping distance must fit within the reliable sensing range | Cap speed from v·t + v²/(2a) + margin < confidence-weighted depth range |
| No minimum-separation guard | Doubling back closes 8 m in ~0.7 s; runners out-brake the drone ([Harper](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/57400/1/57400%20Harper%20et%20al.%20VOR.pdf)) | Hard separation floor that commands brake/climb, overriding the distance PID; feed-forward target velocity |
| Gains tuned in a delay-free sim | Delay halves the gain margin (3.1→1.4) ([2209.01933](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.01933.pdf)) | Inject measured latency; timestamp detections at capture time; propagate the Kalman filter to now + actuation delay |
| Flat-ground foot-point projection | Range error Δh/tanθ on slopes and stairs | Fuse with a range estimate from box height; innovation gate on step changes; target-relative height reference |
| Battery failsafe by percentage | PX4 already offers remaining-flight-time Return ([PX4 Safety](https://docs.px4.io/v1.15/en/config/safety.html)) | Compute RTH energy continuously from distance and estimated wind |
| Supervisor may not notice PX4 taking over | Failsafe RTL overrides offboard control | Monitor flight mode through MAVSDK; switch to RTH/HOLD and stop streaming setpoints |

## Conclusion

The research changes what "following" means for this system. The main task is keeping the right person's identity while the target cannot be seen, with safe bounded motion until it is seen again. The flight states built around a visible target matter less than the gap between losing sight and re-locking. That makes REACQUIRING the most safety-critical state in the supervisor, not a recovery feature. It is the state where the Phantom 4 Pro veered into trees, and where Skydio and DJI drones picked up the wrong person.

The second lesson concerns the simulator itself. Ground-truth depth, zero latency and clean renders make it test a drone that does not exist. Some important things remain unknown. No source gives a validated prediction horizon for re-locking a pedestrian, any metric accuracy for Depth Anything V2 by distance at low drone altitude, or real failure rates for commercial follow modes. The simulator can produce the first two numbers itself, as long as the degraded-depth and injected-latency modes are always on. Passing scenarios 1–6 in degraded mode is a better sign of readiness for real flight than passing all twenty with ground truth.
